Then use your new ability to reach the next part.

With all eight parts restored, you find the rock that shows water once soaked this place: the hematite blueberries.

Built for 90 days. She lasted nearly 15 years.

Opportunity, “Oppy” to her team, landed on Mars on January 24, 2004. She was built to work for 90 Martian days. She kept going for nearly 15 years and drove 28.06 miles (45.16 km), the record for distance driven on another world until Perseverance passed it in October 2026.

In her first weeks, inside little Eagle Crater, she found clues that this place had once been soaking wet: salty, layered rock, a mineral called jarosite that needs water to form, and gray spheres the size of BBs, scattered by the thousands. The team nicknamed them blueberries.

In June 2018, a dust storm wrapped the whole planet and her solar panels went dark. Engineers sent more than 1,000 commands to wake her. On February 12, 2019, while Billie Holiday’s “I’ll Be Seeing You” played in mission control, they sent the last ones. She never answered.

She’s still out there, parked in Perseverance Valley on the rim of Endeavour Crater. Most young space fans only hear about the rovers working today. We wanted them to meet Oppy, and to make one of her biggest discoveries for themselves.

  1. July 7, 2003Launches from Cape Canaveral, Florida.
  2. Jan. 24, 2004Lands in Eagle Crater on Meridiani Planum, a plain chosen because orbiters had spotted hematite there.
  3. Mar. 2, 2004The team announces that the rocks around her were once soaking wet.
  4. Mar. 18, 2004Her Mössbauer spectrometer confirms the blueberries are made of hematite.
  5. Aug. 9, 2011Reaches Endeavour Crater after a drive of nearly three years and 13 miles (21 km).

Parts restored: 1 of 8

Rocker-bogie wheels

In the game

You wake up unable to move, seeing only black and white through your front and rear hazard cameras. Your wheels are buried right beside you. Digging them up teaches you how repairs work, and gets you rolling.

On the real rover

Six wheels, each with its own motor. The front and rear wheels could steer, so Oppy could turn in place. Her rocker-bogie suspension let her roll over rocks and through holes bigger than a wheel, about 10 inches (25 cm) across, while her body moved only half as much as her wheels. Top speed on flat, hard ground: 2 inches (5 cm) per second.

Unlocks driving

The mast: Navcam and Pancam

In the game

Two parts are buried nearby. Navcam lets you look all the way around you. Pancam brings back color.

On the real rover

Oppy’s head sat on a mast about 5 feet (1.5 m) up, roughly a person’s eye level. Navcam’s twin black-and-white cameras took 3D pictures 45° wide so drivers could plan safe routes. Pancam’s twin cameras saw a narrower 16° view in much more detail.

Their sensors were black and white too. Each Pancam eye had a wheel of eight filters, and color pictures were built by combining shots taken through different filters.

Unlocks a 360° view and color

Mini-TES

In the game

Scan rocks from far away and find the one richest in hematite, the iron mineral the blueberries are made of. The scan tells you where to drive.

On the real rover

Every rock gives off heat, and every mineral gives off infrared light in its own pattern, like a fingerprint. Mini-TES read those patterns from a distance, so the team could choose which rocks were worth the drive. It was tuned to look for minerals that form in water.

Hematite spotted from orbit by its bigger sibling, the TES instrument on Mars Global Surveyor, is the main reason Oppy was sent to Meridiani Planum.

Unlocks mineral scans from far away

The instrument arm

In the game

Four tools are buried around the site. Each one becomes its own minigame: grind the rock, spot the round grains, hold the APXS steady, and read the Mössbauer graph. See the minigames.

On the real rover

Oppy’s arm moved much like yours, with a shoulder, elbow, and wrist. A turret at its end held four tools:

Rock abrasion tool (RAT)
A grinder with diamond teeth, about the size of a soda can. It cut a circle 45 mm (1.8 in) across and about 5 mm deep, then brushed it clean, so the other tools could study fresh rock instead of dusty crust. Hard volcanic rock took about two hours.
Microscopic Imager
A close-up camera that worked like a geologist’s hand lens. Its black-and-white pictures show about 30 micrometers per pixel, enough to see the blueberries sitting in the rock.
Alpha particle X-ray spectrometer (APXS)
Aimed alpha particles and X-rays from a tiny curium-244 source at a rock, then counted the X-rays that came back. Each element answers at its own energy, revealing what the rock is made of, including how much iron it holds. A good reading took 10 hours or more, and most were taken at night.
Mössbauer spectrometer
Used gamma rays from cobalt-57 to identify minerals that contain iron. At a spot nicknamed Berry Bowl, it found six peaks, the fingerprint of hematite. Weeks earlier it had found jarosite, a mineral that needs water to form. One measurement took about 12 hours.
Unlocks up-close science

The blueberries

In the game

With every part back, you bring all your tools to one rock and confirm one of Opportunity’s biggest discoveries.

On the real rover

The blueberries are BB-sized spheres of hematite, more gray than blue, packed into the rock and scattered across the ground. They’re concretions: they grew inside rock soaked with groundwater that carried iron. Some are fused in a row, which fits that story. Making them took many times more water than the volume of the rock they sit in.

Why “blueberries”? In false-color pictures, the gray spheres look blue next to the rusty soil. Try the color switch under the view.

Confirmed as hematite on March 18, 2004

1. Grind: rock abrasion tool

How it plays

Pick a spot on a hematite-rich rock. A target circle sweeps around it, and you follow it: while your cursor is inside the circle the grinding bar fills, and when you slip out it drains. Then you brush the dust away with the same move.

Last, look for small gray dots in the fresh circle. Some gray dots and decoys already poke out of the rock before you grind. No dots inside the circle? Try another rock.

The real tool

The real RAT ground a circle 45 mm across and about 5 mm deep, then brushed it clean so the other tools could study fresh rock. Hard volcanic rock took about two hours.

2. Look: Microscopic Imager

How it plays

Zoom into the circle you just ground and spot every possible round grain. They hide among pebbles, dust clumps, and cracks. A real round grain has all three traits: perfectly round, the same size as the others, and smooth. Every decoy is missing at least one, and if you pick a decoy, the game tells you which trait it fails.

The game calls them “round grains” here on purpose. Players don’t learn they’re the famous blueberries until the end.

The real tool

The real Microscopic Imager took black-and-white close-ups at about 30 micrometers per pixel. At Eagle Crater the spheres were 3 to 6 millimeters across, about the size of BBs.

Hold to push the white bar right, let go to let it slide back. Keep it inside the blue zone.

Web demo. AI-generated illustration.

3. Measure: APXS

How it plays

Place the APXS over the ground circle and keep it steady with a fishing-style bar. Hold to push your bar one way, let go and it drifts back. Inside the moving zone, the instrument holds still and the scan fills. Outside it, the instrument starts to stray from its mark.

A finished scan reads either “Iron-rich, consistent with hematite” or “No iron boost here. These grains are something else.” No iron boost means back to another rock: grind and look again.

The real tool

The real APXS was pressed against the rock by the arm and counted the X-rays coming back. A good reading took 10 hours or more, and most were taken at night.

4. Identify: Mössbauer spectrometer

How it plays

Scan the same spot again and read the graph. Two peaks mean olivine or pyroxene, volcanic minerals that also contain iron. If you see that, it’s back to a new rock. Six peaks mean hematite.

Case closed: the round grains are hematite, the same mineral the orbiter saw from space. Only now does the game reveal they’re the blueberries.

The real tool

On Mars, Opportunity’s Mössbauer found olivine and pyroxene mostly in dark volcanic sand, and hematite’s six peaks at Berry Bowl. The rover measured radiation bouncing back off the rock, so its graphs show peaks pointing up.

How we made it

We modeled every rover part in the game ourselves in SolidWorks, then built the game around those models in Godot.

  1. 1Research the real part

    We read NASA and science-team descriptions of each instrument: what it looked like, how it worked, and what it found.

  2. 2Model it in CAD

    We built each part as a 3D model in SolidWorks, working from NASA photos and diagrams of the real rover.

  3. 3Bring it into the game

    Each model spins at the top of the dig screen, next to an animation of the part at work.

  4. 4Write the fact

    Every dig sentence teaches one real thing about that part.

What’s real, and what we changed

Accuracy matters to us, so here is every place where the game bends reality, next to what actually happened.

In our gameOn the real Opportunity

Before the mast is fixed, you see only black and white, front and back.True to life. The four Hazcams sat low on the body, two facing forward and two facing back, and took black-and-white fisheye pictures about 120° wide.

Who it’s for

Kids and casual space fans

No science background needed. You learn each instrument the moment you need it, so every fact arrives as a new ability.

Learning by doing

Players don’t just read facts. They type them to dig, then play a minigame with each tool, in the same order the real science team used them.

A machine worth remembering

Oppy is still sitting on Mars. We want players to finish the game feeling like they know her, and curious about every robot we’ve left out there.
